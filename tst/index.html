What the interviews probably look like

Capital One calls its final round a Power Day: three or four back-to-back interviews of about an hour each (Exponent). Engineering loops usually include coding, system design, a business case and a behavioral interview. Senior and lead candidates may get a second system design round instead of the case (IGotAnOffer).

The design prompts lean heavily toward banking and cards. "Design a banking system" is the most reported question across levels. Others include card authorization, account balances, payment processing, fraud pipelines and mobile banking backends (Interview Query, PracHub).

This role is more architecture-heavy than a normal engineering loop. The posting puts high-level designs (HLDs), architecture decision records (ADRs), Architecture Review Board work, a capability catalog and business partnership at the centre. Expect at least one conversation to test how you run architecture, not only how you draw it.

Ask your recruiter one question early

Public reports for Senior Staff and architect-level loops are thin, and a November 2025 Toronto principal candidate reported a coding screen first (Taro). Ask: "How many design rounds are there, is there an architecture case or presentation, and is there a coding screen?" Then shift your weekly hours to match.

What "Senior Staff" looks like in the room

A senior candidate produces a solid design with some guidance. At staff and above, interviewers look for someone who drives the conversation, finds the hard part quickly, decides, and treats the interviewer as a peer (Hello Interview, IGotAnOffer). The 45-minute method

You need a route you can run under pressure without thinking about it. This one has five stages. The fourth stage, where you go deep on the hard part, is where the staff-level signal lives, so the first three must be quick.

1 · Frame (0–5 min)

Your goal is to agree on what "correct" means before you design anything. Ask a few sharp questions, state your assumptions as numbers, then name the crux out loud.

  • Who and what: who uses it, and which three flows matter most? Say what you're leaving out.
  • How big: users, transactions per second at average and peak, data retention.
  • How good: latency target, availability target, recovery time (RTO) and data-loss tolerance (RPO).
  • What rules: card data (PCI), personal information (PIPEDA), audit retention, data residency.

Banking requirements checklist: say these without being asked

  1. Correctness: no money lost, duplicated or half-applied.
  2. Consistency: per-account strong consistency where decisions depend on balance; eventual is fine for views.
  3. Idempotency: every money-moving request is safe to retry.
  4. Auditability: who did what, when, and why the system decided what it did; immutable and retained.
  5. Security and privacy: data classification, encryption, least privilege, tokenized card numbers.
  6. Resilience: availability target, RTO and RPO per flow, behaviour when a dependency fails.
  7. Operability and cost: SLOs, observability, safe deployment, cost per transaction.
I'll assume 10 million cards, about 2,000 authorizations a second on average and 10,000 at peak, with a 150 ms budget inside our systems. The hard part is keeping available credit correct under concurrent authorizations while staying inside that budget, so I'll spend most of our time there.

2 · Shape (5–10 min)

  • Entities: four to six nouns with their keys, such as Account (account_id), Authorization (auth_id, network_txn_id) and JournalEntry.
  • API: three to five calls. Put the idempotency key in money-moving calls. Derive the caller's identity from the token, never from the request body.
  • Numbers: only the estimates that change a decision. For example, 10,000 per second times 3 KB is about 30 MB/s of writes, so one relational primary is borderline and partitioning matters.

3 · Sketch (10–22 min)

Draw the simplest design that handles the functional requirements end to end, then walk one request through it with numbered steps. Don't add caches, queues or regions yet; mention where they might go and move on.

  • Lay it out left to right: clients, then the edge, then services, then data.
  • Solid arrows are synchronous calls. Dashed arrows are asynchronous events.
  • Write what each data store holds and its key next to it, such as "holds · PK = account_id".
  • Draw trust boundaries as dashed boxes: the card-data (PCI) zone, the VPC, a partner edge.

4 · Deepen the crux (22–38 min)

Pick two or three deep dives driven by the crux you named in stage 1. For each one, run the same four beats: options → decision → why → the risk you accept and how you'd watch it.

Module 01 · Week 1 · about 6 hours

Trade-offs, numbers and SLOs

Every answer at this level is a trade-off defended with a number. This module gives you the arithmetic to do that in your head while you talk.

Why it matters here

Capital One engineers talk publicly about engineering authorization-grade systems for five nines (see the re:Invent 2025 talk in the reading list). An interviewer will expect you to turn "highly available" into a number and show what it costs.

Core ideas

Tail latency, not the average

If one request fans out to 10 services and each has a 1% chance of being slow, about 10% of requests hit at least one slow call (1 − 0.99¹⁰ ≈ 9.6%). Fan-out turns rare slowness into a common experience, so you design and alert on P99, and you cap fan-out on the critical path.

I'll budget P99 per hop, because with this fan-out the average tells us nothing about what a customer feels.

Availability composes

Dependencies in series multiply: three 99.9% services in a chain give about 99.7%. Redundant copies in parallel give 1 − (1 − a)ⁿ, so two independent 99.9% replicas give 99.9999%. The catch is "independent": shared deployments, shared config and shared regions fail together. The useful moves are taking dependencies off the critical path (async, fallback) and making redundancy truly independent.

Little's Law sizes everything

Requests in flight = arrival rate × time in system. At 10,000 per second and 150 ms, you have 1,500 requests in flight. That number sizes thread pools, database connections and Lambda concurrency. It also explains outages: if a dependency slows to 2 s, in-flight requests jump to 20,000 and you run out of everything at once.

SLIs, SLOs and error budgets

An SLI is what you measure (the share of authorizations answered in under 150 ms). An SLO is the target (99.95% per 30 days). The error budget is what's left: 0.05% of a 30-day month is 21.6 minutes. When the budget is spent, you slow releases and fix reliability. This turns reliability arguments into a shared rule that product and engineering both accept.

Estimates that change decisions

Only estimate what moves a decision: peak writes per second (one primary, or partition?), storage growth (hot store, or archive tier?), in-flight concurrency (pool sizes, quotas). Round hard and say so.

Consistency vs availability, honestly

The CAP theorem only bites during a network partition: then you choose between refusing requests and serving possibly stale data. Most of the time the real trade-off is latency vs consistency (PACELC). In a bank, choose consistency for decisions that move money, and availability plus freshness indicators for views.

Explain it back

  1. Why does fan-out make P99 more important than the average?
  2. Two replicas at 99.9% each: when is the combined availability 99.9999%, and when is it closer to 99.9%?
  3. Use Little's Law to explain how one slow dependency can exhaust a service's connection pool.
  4. Explain an error budget to a product manager in two sentences.

Drills

D1 · Storage estimate for card authorizations

3,000 authorizations per second on average, peak 4×. Each writes a 1 KB decision record and emits a 2 KB event. Kept one year with 3× replication. What are the peak rate, daily data and yearly storage?

Answer key

Peak = 12,000/s. Daily = 3,000 × 86,400 = 259.2 M records × 3 KB ≈ 778 GB/day. Yearly ≈ 284 TB, × 3 replication ≈ 850 TB. Decision: hot store keeps 13 months for disputes and service; older data moves to S3 with lifecycle rules and Object Lock for audit.

D2 · Concurrency at peak

12,000/s at a P99 of 150 ms. How much concurrency do you need, and what AWS default would bite if this ran on Lambda?

Answer key

12,000 × 0.15 = 1,800 in flight, plus headroom (aim for ~3,000). Lambda's default regional concurrency quota is 1,000, so you'd raise it and use reserved concurrency for this function so other workloads can't starve it. At steady high throughput, also compare cost against Fargate.

D3 · Availability of an authorization path

API Gateway 99.99%, authorization service 99.95%, DynamoDB 99.99%, fraud service 99.9%, all in series. What's the path availability, and what's the cheapest change to reach 99.9%+?

Answer key

0.9999 × 0.9995 × 0.9999 × 0.999 ≈ 99.83% (about 15 hours a year). Make fraud a soft dependency (60 ms timeout, rules-only fallback): the chain becomes ≈ 99.93%. Beyond that you need redundancy at the service and data level across regions.

D4 · Error budget policy

Write a three-line error budget policy for an authorization service with a 99.95% monthly SLO.

Answer key
  1. Budget is 21.6 minutes of SLI failure per 30 days.
  2. Above 50% spent: only fixes and canaried changes ship; on-call reviews every release.
  3. Above 100%: feature freeze until the 30-day window recovers; postmortem actions get top priority.

Module 02 · Week 2 · about 8 hours

Data: storage, replication, partitioning and consistency

Most banking design mistakes are data mistakes: the wrong store, the wrong key, or a concurrency anomaly nobody noticed. Learn to pick stores from access patterns and to name the anomaly before it bites.

Core ideas

Choose the store from access patterns

List the reads and writes first, then pick. Relational (Aurora PostgreSQL) for multi-row transactions, constraints and ad-hoc queries. DynamoDB for known access patterns with predictable latency at any scale, conditional writes and small transactions. OpenSearch for text search. S3 for documents, archives and the data lake. A cache only for data whose staleness you can tolerate.

The access patterns are: get balance by account, list transactions by account and time, search by merchant name. The first two fit a key-value model keyed by account; search goes to OpenSearch, fed from a change stream.

Replication and replica lag

Leader–follower is the default. With asynchronous followers, a read right after a write can miss it. Fixes: read from the leader after a write (read-your-writes), pass a version or LSN token, or accept staleness and show "as of" times. Multi-leader brings write conflicts; leaderless quorums need R + W > N for overlap.

Partitioning and hot keys

The partition key decides your scaling and your hot spots. Hash keys spread load; range keys support ordered scans. In a bank the natural key is account_id, but corporate accounts with thousands of cards sharing one limit become hot. DynamoDB caps each partition at roughly 1,000 write units and 3,000 read units per second, so a hot account needs a design: split counters, credit tranches across sub-partitions, or a dedicated store.

Isolation anomalies with money

Lost update: two withdrawals read a $100 balance, both subtract $80, both write. Under PostgreSQL's default Read Committed isolation, this read-then-write pattern is exposed. Write skew: two transactions each check a rule across rows ("combined balance stays above zero"), each writes a different row, and the rule breaks. Fixes: atomic conditional updates, SELECT … FOR UPDATE, optimistic version columns, or SERIALIZABLE isolation with retries.

-- Atomic, safe under concurrency: the check and the write are one statement
UPDATE accounts
   SET available = available - :amount, version = version + 1
 WHERE account_id = :id AND available >= :amount;
-- 0 rows updated  →  decline (insufficient funds)

# DynamoDB equivalent
UpdateItem  Key={pk: "ACCT#123"}
  UpdateExpression    "SET available = available - :amt"
  ConditionExpression "available >= :amt"

Consistency from the customer's seat

Linearizable: after the transfer returns, every read sees it. Read-your-writes: you see your change immediately, others may lag. Monotonic reads: you never see time go backwards between refreshes. Eventual: the search index may trail by seconds. Choose per screen, and show freshness where you choose eventual consistency ("Pending", "Updated 10 s ago").

Change data capture feeds everything else

Keep one system of record per entity, and feed read models, search, analytics and caches from its change stream (DynamoDB Streams, or Debezium on PostgreSQL logical replication). This avoids dual writes and gives you replay.

Explain it back

  1. Explain write skew with two joint-account withdrawals.
  2. Why is "read from a replica" dangerous for an authorization decision but fine for the transaction history screen?
  3. What makes a partition key good, and what makes one hot?
  4. Why feed search from a change stream instead of writing to both stores?

Drills

D1 · Key design for transaction history

Access patterns: (a) latest 50 transactions for an account, (b) all transactions for an account in a date range, (c) one transaction by ID for a dispute. Design a DynamoDB key schema.

Answer key

PK = ACCT#<account_id>, SK = TXN#<ISO timestamp>#<txn_id>. (a) and (b) are Query with descending order and a BETWEEN on SK. (c) uses a GSI with PK = TXN#<txn_id>. Archive data older than the hot window to S3 and query it with Athena for older disputes.

D2 · Spot the bug
bal = SELECT available FROM accounts WHERE id = :id
if bal >= amount:
    UPDATE accounts SET available = :bal - :amount WHERE id = :id
    INSERT INTO holds (...)

What goes wrong under concurrency, and how do you fix it?

Answer key

Lost update: two requests read the same balance and both write. Fix with one conditional update (shown above), in the same transaction as the hold insert, or with SELECT … FOR UPDATE. Also add an idempotency key so retries don't create a second hold.

D3 · The hot corporate account

A corporate account has 4,000 employee cards sharing one $2M limit, with peaks of 2,000 authorizations a second. DynamoDB partitions cap at about 1,000 writes a second. Design it.

Answer key

Split the open-to-buy into N tranches (e.g. 8 sub-items, $250K each), and route each card to a tranche by hash. Each authorization does a conditional decrement on its tranche. A rebalancer moves headroom between tranches asynchronously. When one tranche is empty, try a second tranche before declining. Accept that the account can be declined slightly before the full limit is reached; agree that tolerance with the business. Name the alternative: a single-writer actor per account in memory, which gives exact accounting but needs careful failover.

Module 03 · Week 3 · about 10 hours · highest priority

Money correctness: ledgers, idempotency, sagas, reconciliation

This is the crux of most Capital One prompts. If you can show money is never lost, duplicated or half-applied, and that every movement is explainable later, you've passed the hardest part of a banking design.

Core ideas

Double-entry ledger

Every movement is a journal entry whose lines balance: total debits equal total credits. Entries are append-only and immutable; you correct mistakes with a new reversing entry, never an update. A balance is the sum of an account's lines. You store a materialized balance for speed, but it's updated in the same transaction as the entry, so the two can't disagree. Internal accounts (fees, suspense, network settlement) make every flow balance.

Entry: customer A pays $50 to customer BAccountDebitCredit
line 1A · deposit account50.00
line 2B · deposit account50.00

For deposit accounts the bank owes the customer, so a debit reduces A's balance and a credit increases B's.

Ledger balance vs available balance

Ledger (posted) balance is what has settled. Available balance subtracts holds and pending items. For a card: available credit = limit − posted balance − open authorizations. Decisions use available; statements use posted. Mixing the two is a classic interview mistake.

Idempotency keys

The client sends a unique key with each money-moving request. The server stores the key, a hash of the request and the result, in the same transaction as the effect. A retry with the same key returns the stored result. The same key with a different body is rejected (HTTP 422). A request still in progress gets a 409 or waits on a lock. Keys expire after a window such as 24 hours.

"Exactly once" means at-least-once delivery plus idempotent effects

Networks can't deliver exactly once. Messages will be duplicated, so every consumer either dedupes on a message ID or makes its effect naturally idempotent (an upsert keyed by transaction ID).

Transactional outbox

Writing to the database and then publishing an event is a dual write: a crash in between loses the event. Instead, write the event to an outbox table in the same transaction as the state change, and let a relay (polling or CDC) publish it. You get at-least-once events that never contradict the database.

Sagas instead of distributed transactions

When a flow spans services or external rails, two-phase commit blocks on a coordinator and is impossible across organisations. A saga is a sequence of local transactions with compensations (a reversing entry, a hold release). Orchestrated sagas (Step Functions or a state-machine service) are easier to audit; choreographed ones (services reacting to events) suit simple fan-outs. Use explicit pending states so the customer sees the truth mid-flow.

Unknown outcomes

The hardest case is when you sent money to an external rail and the call timed out. Did it go through? Never assume it failed. Keep the transfer pending, query the rail's status with your reference, retry with the same idempotent reference, and let reconciliation settle it. Compensate only on a definitive failure.

Reconciliation is part of the design

Compare your ledger with external truth (network clearing files, rail statements, bank settlement reports) every day. Mismatches become exceptions with owners and SLAs. Say it in the interview: "The ledger is only right if it reconciles."

Card lifecycle: authorize, clear, settle

Authorization approves the purchase and places a hold; there's no ledger posting yet. Clearing (presentment) arrives later, typically within a few days, and creates the posting, matching and releasing the hold. Settlement moves funds between banks. Holds that never clear expire after a set number of days.

Explain it back

  1. Why must the idempotency record be written in the same transaction as the effect?
  2. What's the difference between a dual write and the outbox pattern?
  3. A transfer to another bank timed out. Walk through exactly what your system does next.
  4. Why don't authorizations create ledger entries?

Drills

D1 · Write the journal entries

For a credit card: (1) $50 purchase authorized; (2) it clears; (3) $2 foreign transaction fee; (4) the merchant refunds $50; (5) the customer pays $100.

Answer key
  1. No entry. Create a hold that reduces available credit by $50.
  2. Dr Cardholder receivable 50 / Cr Network settlement payable 50; release the hold.
  3. Dr Cardholder receivable 2 / Cr Fee income 2.
  4. Dr Network settlement payable 50 / Cr Cardholder receivable 50.
  5. Dr Cash (payment clearing) 100 / Cr Cardholder receivable 100. The account now shows a $48 credit balance.
D2 · Retry while in flight

A mobile client times out after 3 s and retries POST /transfers with the same key while the first request is still running. Design the behaviour.

Answer key

Insert the key with status IN_PROGRESS using a conditional put; the second request fails the condition and reads the record. If still in progress, return 409 with Retry-After (or wait briefly on it). When complete, return the stored response. If the first request crashed, a lease timeout on IN_PROGRESS lets a later retry take over safely, because the effect itself is also guarded by the transaction.

D3 · Design reconciliation

Design daily reconciliation between your card ledger and the network's clearing file.

Answer key

Ingest the file to S3 (immutable), parse to records, and match on network reference, amount and date. Outcomes: matched; in file but not in ledger (missing posting, so post it via the normal path); in ledger but not in file (possible duplicate or late file, so hold for N days); amount mismatch (exception queue). Track a break count and value SLI, alert on thresholds, and give exceptions an owner and an ageing report. Keep everything replayable and idempotent so you can re-run a day.

Ordering is per key, and that's enough

Failures in consumers

Retry transient errors with exponential backoff and jitter. Send messages that keep failing to a dead-letter queue, with an alert and a redrive tool. On an ordered log, one poison message blocks its partition, so park it and move on if the business allows.

Backpressure and load levelling

A queue absorbs spikes so downstream systems see a steady rate. Watch the age of the oldest message; it's a better SLI than queue length. Scale consumers on it, but cap concurrency to protect the database behind them.

Events are facts in the past tense (CardAuthorized, TransferSettled). Use an envelope: event ID, type, schema version, time, source, subject ID and correlation ID. Evolve schemas compatibly (add optional fields; never rename or repurpose). Keep PII out: carry tokens and IDs, not names and card numbers. Every topic has one owning team.

Reads, caching and traffic

Banks read far more than they write: balances, history, offers, dashboards. The skill is knowing what you may cache, how stale it can be, and how to protect the system when traffic surges.

Core ideas

Cache patterns

Cache-aside (the app reads the cache, falls back to the database, fills the cache) is the default. Read-through and write-through push that logic into the cache layer. Write-behind acknowledges before the database has the data; avoid it for money. Invalidate with TTLs for simple cases and with change events where staleness matters.

What not to cache in a bank

Never make a money decision from a cache. The authorization check and the transfer's funds check read the system of record. Caching is fine for display ("Available $1,240 · updated 20 s ago"), offer catalogs, product rules and reference data.

The home screen balance can come from a cache with a short TTL and an "as of" time. The transfer flow always reads the ledger, because that's where the money decision is made.

Stampedes and hot keys

When a popular key expires, thousands of requests miss at once. Use request coalescing (one loader, the rest wait), jittered TTLs and early refresh. For hot keys, replicate them across cache nodes or add a small in-process cache.

Rate limiting

A token bucket allows bursts up to the bucket size and a sustained rate equal to the refill rate. Limit at the edge (API Gateway usage plans per partner) and inside the service (per customer, per card). Distributed counters live in Redis or DynamoDB. Return 429 with Retry-After. For partners, limits belong in the contract.

Load balancing, pooling and pagination

Layer 7 load balancers route by path and header and do health checks. Pool database connections (RDS Proxy in front of Aurora when Lambda is the client). Paginate with cursors, not offsets, so deep pages stay fast and results don't shift.

Consistent hashing

Map keys and nodes onto a ring so that adding or removing a node only moves about 1/N of the keys. Virtual nodes even out the load. It's how cache clusters and many partitioned stores rebalance without a full reshuffle.

  1. Cache aggressively (hours to days), invalidate on refresh events.
  2. Cache 15–60 s with an "as of" label, and evict on balance-change events.
  3. Never cache. Read the system of record atomically.
  4. Cache at the CDN and in the app for hours; invalidate on publish.

Reliability, resilience and multi-region

Capital One has given at least five public talks on resilience. Expect to be pushed on what happens when things break, how fast you recover, and what that costs.

Core ideas

Timeouts, retries, backoff and jitter

Every remote call gets a timeout derived from the caller's own budget. Retry only idempotent operations, with exponential backoff plus random jitter so clients don't retry in lockstep. Retries multiply load at every layer, so retry at one layer and use a retry budget (for example, retries at most 10% of requests).

Circuit breakers, bulkheads and load shedding

A circuit breaker stops calling a failing dependency and fails fast. Bulkheads give each dependency its own pool so one slow call can't consume every thread. Load shedding rejects excess work early (cheaply) instead of accepting it and timing out everything.

Graceful degradation: hard vs soft dependencies

Decide which dependencies are hard (the request fails without them) and turn as many as possible into soft ones. If fraud scoring is slow, fall back to rules with a lower risk appetite. If the notification service is down, authorize anyway and send later. The fallback policy is a business decision; bring the business into it.

Limit the blast radius

Use multiple Availability Zones by default. Cells are independent copies of the stack, each serving a slice of customers, so a bad deploy or poison input hurts one cell. Shuffle sharding gives each customer a random subset of workers, so one noisy customer rarely takes down another. Separate AWS accounts add security and quota isolation.

Multi-region and recovery targets

RTO is how long you may be down; RPO is how much data you may lose. AWS describes four strategies from cheap and slow to expensive and instant (DR whitepaper). Choose per workload, not per company.

StrategyRTORPOCostFits
Backup and restoreHours+Last backupLowestStatement archive, internal reporting

Warm standbyMinutesLowMediumCredit monitoring, servicing apps

Active/active without conflicts

Two regions writing the same balance will conflict. The usual pattern is a home region per account: all writes for an account go to its home, replicated asynchronously to the other region. On regional failure you move the home, accepting the replication lag as bounded risk (for example, capping authorization amounts during failover). Fail over with a data-plane mechanism you've practised (Route 53 Application Recovery Controller), not a console click, and design for static stability: the surviving region should already have the capacity it needs.

Most outages start with a change

Deploy with canaries by slice (a percentage of traffic, a BIN range), watch SLOs and business signals, and roll back automatically. Feature flags separate deploy from release. Capital One's Credit Offers API team used canary deployments to release around the clock without disrupting affiliates.

Observability and game days

Track RED metrics (rate, errors, duration) per service, distributed traces with a correlation ID from the edge, structured logs with no PII, and business SLIs. An approval-rate drop is often your first sign of an incident. Practise failure deliberately with chaos experiments and region-evacuation game days.

Security, privacy and compliance architecture

In a bank, security isn't a section at the end; it shapes the boundaries of the design. Your HIPAA and GDPR work transfers directly. Learn the Canadian banking vocabulary and the card-data specifics.

Core ideas

Threat model the data flow

Draw the flow, mark trust boundaries, then walk STRIDE at each boundary: Spoofing, Tampering, Repudiation, Information disclosure, Denial of service, Elevation of privilege. Ten minutes of this in an interview reads as senior.

Identity everywhere

Customers: OpenID Connect, MFA, device binding, and step-up authentication for risky actions (adding a payee, changing a phone number). Services: short-lived credentials (IAM roles, mTLS), never shared secrets. Staff: SSO and just-in-time access with approval and logging. Authorization: role- or attribute-based, ideally in a policy engine you can audit.

Least privilege: the 2019 lesson

Capital One's 2019 breach is public and well analysed: a misconfigured web application firewall allowed server-side request forgery, which reached the EC2 instance metadata service and obtained credentials for a role with broad S3 access (KrebsOnSecurity). Architectural lessons: narrowly scoped roles, IMDSv2, egress controls, S3 access through VPC endpoints and bucket policies, and anomaly detection on data access. Capital One also created Cloud Custodian, an open-source policy-as-code tool for cloud guardrails. Mention lessons respectfully and briefly if it's relevant.

Protecting data

TLS in transit. Encryption at rest with KMS. Envelope encryption: a data key encrypts the data and a KMS key encrypts the data key, so keys rotate cheaply and access is auditable. Field-level encryption for the most sensitive fields. Tokenization replaces the card number with a token so most systems never touch real card data, which shrinks PCI DSS scope; encryption alone doesn't (Stripe). Classify data, minimise it, and mask it in logs.

Audit trails

Record who did what, when, from where, and why the system decided. Make records immutable (S3 Object Lock, a separate security account), keep them for the retention period, and make them searchable for investigations.

The anatomy of a high-level design

  1. Context and goals: the business outcome, with a measure.
  2. Scope and non-goals.

How review boards work, from both sides

As presenter: in 10 minutes cover the problem, the constraints, two or three options, your decision, the risks and what you need from the board. As reviewer: check the requirements trace to the design, the failure modes and recovery targets, security and data classification, standards and exceptions, cost, and operational ownership. Prefer approve with conditions and time-limited exceptions over rejection. Automate standards as fitness functions and policy-as-code so the board reviews judgment, not checklists.

Capability maps

A business capability is what the business does, independent of how: acquire customers, underwrite, issue cards, authorize, post transactions, bill, collect, service customers, report to regulators. Map systems onto capabilities to see duplication, gaps and where investment should go. Shared capabilities (decisioning, identity, notifications) become platform candidates.

Build or buy

Score options on differentiation (does this make us better than competitors?), time to value, total cost over 3–5 years, integration effort, regulatory and vendor risk (OSFI B-10), and exit cost. Buy the commodity, build the differentiator, and always write down the exit plan.

Migrating without a big bang

The strangler fig pattern routes traffic through a facade and moves capability by capability from legacy to new (Fowler). Supporting techniques: change data capture to keep data in sync, parallel run with automated diffing, dark launches, feature-flag cutover by slice, and a rollback for every step. Think in transition architectures, each a stable state you could stop at.

Communicating at every level

Gregor Hohpe's "architect elevator": the same decision told three ways. To executives, talk risk, cost and time to market. To product, talk capabilities and options. To engineers, talk the design and its trade-offs. Practise telling one decision all three ways.

ML and AI systems in a bank

Capital One integrates machine learning and conversational AI into core operations, and a reported Senior+ question asks for an agent platform with guardrails. This is where your Roseline and Curacel work becomes an advantage. Frame it as architecture and governance, not model tuning.

Core ideas

Real-time scoring on a latency budget

The model call is usually the smallest part. Getting fresh features is the hard part: real-time aggregates (spend in the last hour, new merchant, distance from the last transaction) come from stream processing into an online store, and historical features come from an offline store. Keep training and serving features identical (one feature definition, two materialisations) to avoid training-serving skew. Always have a rules-only fallback.

Fraud is a loop, not a model

Rules and models make the decision; case management handles reviews; confirmed fraud and chargebacks come back weeks later as labels. Thresholds are business decisions trading fraud loss against declined good customers. Run new models in shadow mode first, then champion/challenger.

Model governance

Regulated banks keep a model inventory, validate models independently, document them, monitor drift and performance, and can reproduce any decision (inputs, feature versions, model version, threshold). Your ML platform's data versioning and reproducible experiments are exactly this.

Agent and LLM platforms with guardrails

A gateway in front of all model calls handles authentication, routing, PII redaction, cost and latency budgets, caching and logging. Retrieval respects the user's entitlements. Tools are explicit, versioned APIs with least-privilege scopes; risky tools need confirmation or human approval. Inputs and outputs pass policy checks (prompt injection, data leakage, unsafe advice). Every turn is audited. Evaluation suites gate every model or prompt change, with a clear human handoff path. These are all things you built for Roseline; present them as a platform.

Worked solution S1 · the most reported question type

Design a credit card authorization system

The issuer's side of a card purchase: a request arrives from the card network, and you approve or decline within the network's deadline without ever over-committing credit.

Reported · Interview Query, PracHub, IGotAnOfferModules 2, 3, 6, 7

Attempt it first

Run the full 45 minutes on this question before reading further. Then compare, stage by stage. The value is in the gap between your attempt and this one.

Frame

Questions to ask: credit cards only? Card-present and online? Tokenized wallets? Do we own fraud scoring or call a platform? How many regions?

Assumptions to state: 10 million active cards. 2,000 authorizations a second on average, 10,000 at peak. P99 of 150 ms inside our systems; the network's deadline is hard, and beyond it the network may apply stand-in processing using limits we set. 99.99%+ availability. Every decision auditable for seven years.

The crux, said out loud

"Three hard parts: available credit must never be over-committed by concurrent authorizations; fraud scoring has to fit inside the latency budget; and if we're unavailable, the network decides for us, so availability is direct financial risk."

Shape

Entities: Card (token; the real number lives in the vault), Account (credit limit, posted balance, open-to-buy), Authorization (auth_id, network_txn_id, amount, merchant, MCC, status, expiry), Hold, DecisionRecord (inputs, rules fired, model version, score).

POST /authorizations      (internal; the gateway translates ISO 8583)
  { network_txn_id, card_token, amount, currency,
    merchant: { id, mcc, country }, entry_mode, wallet_token? }
  → { decision: APPROVE|DECLINE|PARTIAL, auth_code, reason_code }
POST /authorizations/{id}/reversals
POST /advices             (stand-in decisions the network made for us)

Numbers that matter: 10,000/s × 150 ms ≈ 1,500 in flight. At about 3 KB per decision that's ~30 MB/s of writes at peak, so we partition by account from day one.

Sketch

After authorization, clearing files arrive within days. The posting service matches each cleared item to its authorization, writes ledger entries and releases the hold. A scheduler expires holds that never clear.

Deepen

1 · Available credit without over-commit

Options: a row lock in Aurora, a DynamoDB conditional write in a transaction, or an in-memory single writer per account. Decision: DynamoDB keyed by account_id, with one TransactWriteItems that conditionally decrements open-to-buy, inserts the hold and writes the decision record. It's correct by construction, takes single-digit milliseconds and scales by account. Accepted cost: no ad-hoc SQL, so analytics reads the change stream. Hot accounts: credit tranches across sub-items (Module 2, drill 3).

2 · The latency budget

StepP99 budget
Gateway parse and route5 ms
Tokenize card number5 ms
Parallel: account state, card controls, fraud score60 ms (fraud timeout)
Decision rules5 ms
Atomic reserve and write15 ms
Respond5 ms
Total, with ~55 ms headroom~95 ms

No synchronous cross-region calls on this path, and no synchronous calls to anything that isn't needed for the decision.

3 · When fraud scoring is slow or down

Fraud is a soft dependency: a 60 ms timeout and a circuit breaker, then a rules-only fallback with a stricter risk appetite (for example, lower approval ceilings for high-risk merchant categories). The business owns the fallback policy; engineering owns the switch and the alert on fallback rate.

4 · Multi-region

Options: synchronous cross-region writes (correct, but every authorization pays the round trip), or a home region per account with asynchronous replication. Decision: home-region writes, with both regions receiving network traffic and forwarding to the home region. On regional failure the survivor takes over homes and applies a failover limits policy (caps on amounts) to bound the exposure from replication lag. This turns a consistency problem into a bounded, business-approved financial risk, the same idea as network stand-in. Rehearse it with game days.

5 · Idempotency and odd ordering

The network retries with the same network_txn_id, so return the original decision. A reversal can arrive before its authorization; store a reversal marker so the late authorization is cancelled on arrival. Stand-in advices are applied as holds after the fact, and reconciled.

6 · Audit and security

The decision record (inputs, rule-set version, model version, feature snapshot hash, decision) streams to S3 with Object Lock for seven years. The PCI zone is as small as possible: the gateway and the vault. Card verification cryptography runs in HSMs. Services use mTLS and narrowly scoped roles.

Harden and wrap

  • SLIs: P99 decision latency; success rate; approval rate against its forecast (a sudden drop is often the first sign of an incident); stand-in rate; fraud fallback rate.
  • Deployment: canary by a percentage of traffic or BIN range; automatic rollback on approval-rate deviation.
  • At 10×: hot corporate accounts, fraud model latency, stream throughput.
Three decisions: per-account atomic reserves for correctness; fraud as a soft dependency with a business-owned fallback; and home-region writes with bounded-risk failover instead of synchronous cross-region writes.

Design a banking system: accounts, transfers and history

The most reported Capital One question across levels. Scale isn't the hard part; correctness, auditability and honest handling of external rails are.

Reported · Glassdoor, IGotAnOfferModules 2, 3, 4

Attempt it first

This was your Week 1 baseline. Re-attempt it now, then compare.

Frame

Scope: deposit accounts; transfers between accounts at the bank; transfers to other banks through a payment rail; transaction history. Out of scope: lending and cards.

Assumptions: 5 million customers, 10 million transactions a day (~115/s average, ~1,000/s peak). History kept for seven years. 99.95% availability for transfers, 99.9% for history.

The crux

"Money must never be lost, duplicated or half-applied; every balance must be explainable from its entries; and transfers to other banks have outcomes we don't control. At this volume one well-designed ledger database is enough, so I'll spend the time on correctness, not sharding."

Shape

Entities: Customer, Account, LedgerAccount (including internal accounts such as outgoing-transfers clearing), JournalEntry with EntryLines, Transfer (a state machine: CREATED → FUNDS_HELD → SENT → SETTLED, or FAILED → REVERSED), IdempotencyRecord.

journal_entries (entry_id PK, transfer_id, created_at, description)
entry_lines     (entry_id FK, ledger_account_id, direction D|C, amount, currency)
                -- invariant per entry: Σ debits = Σ credits
balances        (ledger_account_id PK, posted, available, version)
                -- updated in the same DB transaction as the lines
transfers       (transfer_id PK, idempotency_key UNIQUE, state, external_ref, …)
outbox          (event_id PK, payload, created_at, published_at)

Sketch

Deepen

1 · Internal transfers need no saga

When both accounts are in the same ledger database, a transfer is a single journal entry in a single transaction. Keeping the ledger in one consistency boundary for as long as scale allows is the simplest correct design. Say this explicitly; it shows you aren't distributing for its own sake.

2 · Concurrency on one account

Use a conditional update on available balance (or SELECT … FOR UPDATE on the balance row), in the same transaction as the entry lines and the idempotency record.

3 · External transfers

An orchestrator (Step Functions Standard, or a state-machine service) holds funds by moving them to an outgoing clearing account, calls the rail with our reference as the idempotency key, and records the outcome. On "unknown" it polls status and waits for reconciliation; it compensates (reverses the entry) only on a definitive failure. The customer sees "Pending" throughout.

4 · History and read models

The outbox feeds a history read model (DynamoDB keyed by account and time) and search. Reads are eventually consistent, with the customer's own new transfer shown immediately from the transfer response (read-your-writes in the client).

5 · Reconciliation and audit

Reconcile the clearing account against the rail's settlement report daily; its balance should return to zero as transfers settle. Every entry carries who initiated it and why; entries are immutable, and corrections are new entries.

6 · When this outgrows one database

Scale vertically and add read replicas first. Next, partition ledger accounts by customer with an internal inter-partition clearing account, so cross-partition transfers become two local entries plus a saga. Mention that purpose-built ledger databases exist, and that you'd evaluate build versus buy.

Design Quick Check: card pre-approval without affecting a credit score

The posting describes Quick Check as giving customers "100% certainty which credit cards they'll be approved for before they apply". That promise is the crux: the pre-approval and the real application must agree.

Product question · likely in job-fit or design roundsModules 7, 9, 10

Frame

Flow: a prospect enters identity and income details and consents; we run a soft credit inquiry (which doesn't affect their score) with a bureau; a decision engine evaluates each card product; we show the products they're pre-approved for; later they apply.

Assumptions: 50,000 checks a day, campaign peaks around 50 per second. The bureau call dominates latency (assume 1–3 s); target under 5 s end to end. 99.9% availability.

The crux

"Honouring the certainty promise; depending on an external bureau we don't control; handling sensitive personal and credit data under consent; and making every decision explainable."

Sketch

Deepen

1 · Keeping the promise

Each pre-approval produces a signed offer token that binds the decision, the policy version and a hash of the inputs, with an expiry. At application, the same engine re-evaluates using the token's policy version. If the applicant's information matches, the outcome matches. The promise's conditions (information unchanged, identity verified) are written into the policy and the customer disclosure.

2 · The bureau as a dependency

A bureau gateway wraps vendor APIs with timeouts, a circuit breaker and contract tests. Options during an outage: tell the customer to try later, or accept the request and notify them by email when it completes. Using a second bureau for resilience means a second policy calibration, so it's a business decision with B-10 third-party-risk implications.

3 · Personal data

Collect the minimum. Record consent with purpose and timestamp. Encrypt bureau responses at field level and keep them only as long as policy allows. Restrict access to the decision service. Analytics uses de-identified data.

4 · Explainable, governed decisions

Store reason codes for every product decision. Policy and model changes go through governance (model risk review and the architecture board) and roll out by version, so any past decision can be reproduced. Experiment freely on presentation; changes to eligibility need approval.

5 · Capability reuse

The same decisioning capability serves the Credit Offers API for affiliates (partner authentication, rate limits, data minimisation in responses). That's a capability-catalog point worth making in an architect interview.

Deck 3 · Review this design

You're on the Architecture Review Board. A team submits this proposal. Find at least eight problems, rank them, and decide: approve, approve with conditions, or reject.

Proposal: "Instant transfers v1"

The mobile app calls API Gateway, which invokes a Lambda function. The function reads the sender's balance from ElastiCache; if it's enough, it writes a debit to DynamoDB table Accounts, then a credit to the recipient's row, in two separate calls. It then publishes a TransferCompleted message to SNS. On any error it retries five times immediately. For debugging, it logs the full request, including account numbers and names. There's no idempotency key; the app disables the button after a tap. The history screen scans the Transactions table filtered by account. Everything runs in one region; backups are nightly.

Reviewer's answer key
  1. Funds check against a cache: stale balances allow overdrafts. Read and decrement atomically at the source of truth.
  2. Two separate writes: a crash between them destroys or creates money. Use one journal entry in one transaction (DynamoDB TransactWriteItems, or a ledger database).
  3. No idempotency: a disabled button doesn't stop network retries. Require an idempotency key stored with the effect.
  4. Dual write to SNS: the event can be lost or describe something that didn't happen. Use an outbox or the change stream.
  5. Immediate retries ×5: retry storms, and duplicate effects without idempotency. Use backoff with jitter, retry only idempotent calls, and set a budget.
  6. PII in logs: a privacy and compliance violation. Use structured logs with tokens and masking.
  7. Scan for history: cost and latency grow with the table. Key by account and time.
  8. One region, nightly backups: RPO up to 24 hours for money movement is unacceptable. Define RTO/RPO; use point-in-time recovery and a multi-region strategy.
  9. No ledger or audit trail: balances can't be explained or reconciled.
  10. No step-up or fraud checks for new recipients or large amounts.

Verdict: reject as submitted, and offer a working session. Items 1–4 are correctness defects, not style. Approve-with-conditions is right only once the money path is fixed.
